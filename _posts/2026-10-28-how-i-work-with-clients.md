Many software problems are business problems in disguise: a report someone
builds by hand every week, a process only one person understands, a system
everyone depends on that has become slow or fragile. My job is to understand
the problem, then build and run the software that fixes it.

This is how I approach that work.

## 1. Understand the problem in your terms

Before I suggest any tool, I want to know how the work happens today: who does
it, how long it takes, what goes wrong and what that costs. A good solution
starts from there, not from the technology.

## 2. Start with the smallest useful thing

Large projects fail slowly. I'd rather find one part of the problem we can
solve quickly, put it in front of the people who use it, and learn from real
use before building more.

## 3. Measure before and after

When my team's automated checks took 40 minutes to run, I measured where the
time went before changing anything. That's how we got them down to 9 minutes.
I take the same approach to any problem: agree on what "better" means, measure
it, then show the result.

## 4. Use AI where it helps, and check its work

I work with AI agents every day. They now write much of the code I ship, and I
review everything they produce. I wrote about
[my setup]({% post_url 2026-10-22-why-i-switched-from-vscode-to-herdr %}).
That has taught me where AI saves real time and where it needs a person to
check it. When I bring AI into a business process, I design it the same way:
automate the repetitive work, and keep a person in charge of the decisions
that matter.

## 5. Build for the long run

Much of my work is on software that has run in production for years,
including a medical imaging platform used in clinical trials. I've brought
long-running applications up to date and added automatic security checks to
catch problems early. What I build should be safe to run for years, not just
on launch day.

## 6. Hand over properly

When a project ends, your team should be able to run it without me. That means
clear documentation, plain explanations of the decisions behind it, and time
with the people who will own it.
